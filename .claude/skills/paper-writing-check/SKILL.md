# Paper writing check

A senior co-author (Martin) read earlier drafts as an outsider and found implicit statements,
in-house jargon and prose that "looks like a summary generated by AI". Juan, the first author,
was embarrassed in front of his supervisors. Shorter text is not the goal; text that someone
outside the project understands on first reading is. Every passage this paper gets must be
understandable by a reader who has not read it, read as a story, and be correct.

Follow the steps in order for every piece of paper text, however small.

## 1. Know the reader

Write for a clinician-scientist or pathologist who knows what survival analysis is but has not
read this paper and does not speak machine-learning jargon. The test for each paragraph: could
Martin, reading it cold, say what it claims and why it matters?

## 2. Keep the story and the framing

Juan fixed the framing; do not drift from it, and do not let a reviewer agent drift it.

- **Story order at every scale** (highlights, abstract, a section, a paragraph): the problem,
  what we did, what happened, what it means.
- **The story of the paper:** pathology AI is judged by benchmark scores, rarely by clinical or
  biological value. We built a reusable tool, the IER audit, to test the explanations AI
  survival models give. On TCGA bladder and breast cancer, the weights matched what the model
  used, but most other explanations failed the tool's checks. These failures show that
  TCGA-sized cohorts (fewer than 200 events) are too small to support such conclusions. The
  failures are evidence of the tool's rigour, not a disappointment.
- **The aim is understanding predictions.** Performance is context. Never frame a result as
  "did not improve performance".
- **Openings are positive:** explanations matter, which is why they need testing.
- **Voice:** "We built a tool", "The tool checks". A sentence or highlight does not start with a
  bare "It".
- Never mention MICCAI or the earlier workshop version. No "then submit" framing.

## 3. Write so an outsider follows

- **Define each term the first time it appears**, in plain words, or replace it. The in-house
  words below have confused readers before:
  - legs: say "checks" (or "the three checks");
  - weight: "a number the model learns for each tissue type, setting how much that tissue
    contributes to the patient's risk";
  - compartment: "tissue type" (as labelled on the slide);
  - tile or patch: "a small square image cut from the slide";
  - faithful: "reflects what the model actually uses";
  - planted signal or confounder: "a known artificial signal added to the data, to see whether
    the explanation finds it";
  - backbone, payoff, coalition, out-of-bag, arm, profile, Shapley value, surrogate, MED3pa,
    concordance or C-index, partial R-squared: define or avoid in the abstract and
    introduction.
- **One claim per sentence.** Put the subject and verb early. Say who did what.
- **Be precise.** Not "for whom the model is reliable" but which patients and how measured; not
  "rarely tested" without saying tested how; not "falls short of trust" without saying what
  was expected.
- **Give a concrete example** wherever an outsider could lose the thread (for instance: a
  heatmap lights up fat, and readers conclude that fat drives the patient's risk).
- **Few numbers in the main text,** each with its meaning; details go to the appendix.
- **Cite what the point needs.** The introduction is a story, not a literature review.
- When a sentence must be inserted into an existing paragraph, read the sentence before and
  after it and make the new one continue the thought; avoid "this" pointing at the wrong thing.

## 4. Remove AI-sounding prose

Before anyone sees the text, go through `references/ai-tells.md` and rewrite every match. The
short version: no formulaic transitions (notably, importantly, moreover, crucially), no default
lists of three, no chains of colons or "X: Y" set-ups, no "not X but Y" for drama, no stated
takeaways ("this highlights", "this underscores"), no vague verbs (survived, leverage,
delve, underscore), no stacked hedges, no em-dash pile-ups, no closing flourish.

## 5. Check every number

Trace every number you write, change or call wrong to its source: `results/*.json` in the repo,
the cluster reports in `docs/` (for example `docs/SHIRIN_CLUSTER_RESULTS.md`), or the current
manuscript. Before saying a number in the paper is wrong, trace it fully; the paper's numbers
have been checked many times. Round as the paper does (for example 0.146 is written 15%).

## 6. Run an outsider reader before Juan sees it

Spawn a fresh subagent with the prompt in `references/reader-prompt.md`. Give it the passage in
its surrounding paragraph, the co-author's comment if there is one, and the facts the passage
rests on. It returns "Understandable without the paper: yes/no", "Reads naturally: yes/no" and
at most five concrete fixes.

- Apply fixes that make the text clearer or more accurate and keep Juan's story and tone.
- Reject fixes that change his framing (for example turning "these failures show the data is
  too small" into "explanations stay hypotheses"), and say that you rejected them.
- Show Juan the final text and list, in a line each, what the reader changed.
- Be honest about what was checked: if text changed after the reader saw it, say so, and
  re-check if the change was more than wording the reader itself proposed.

## 7. Work the way Juan works

- One item at a time. Do not zoom out to the whole to-do list mid-task.
- Short, plain replies. Lead with the answer; give one recommendation, not a menu.
- Show the draft; apply only when he says so.
- Then apply in Overleaf, compile, check for new warnings and undefined references, and
  resolve the co-author's comment with no reply. If he asks for a reply, keep it plain and
  minimal, with no exclamation marks ("Thanks, I added the comments.").
- Ask before pushing to GitHub, posting or anything outward-facing. Juan logs in himself;
  never type a password.
- Overleaf mechanics (editing, compiling, resolving comments) are in `references/overleaf.md`.
